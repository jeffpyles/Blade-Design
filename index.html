    Pareto frontier: hardness vs toughness

    Frontier points are steels no other steel beats on both hardness and toughness. With toughness treated as a constraint, pick the hardest steel at or above the required toughness.

    The argument

    Toughness is a constraint, not an objective

    Steel choice is a trade between hardness (edge retention) and toughness (chip resistance). For a given edge geometry and use, there is a minimum toughness that prevents chipping. Above that, extra toughness buys nothing, so the right steel is the one that reaches the highest hardness at that toughness. Only a handful of alloys on the frontier make sense for most uses.

    The gap

    No published data maps a toughness value (for example Charpy ft-lbs) to a minimum safe edge angle for a given use. Charpy is a bulk test, while edge chipping is driven by crack initiation and low-cycle fatigue at the apex, so the link is unproven. This tool exists to build that map.

    Why hand planes

    In a bevel-down plane the bed sets the cutting angle. The bevel angle changes only edge strength, not cutting geometry, so edge angle is a clean independent variable.

    What the runs show so far

      Test plan

      1. Vary edge angle with microbevels at 15, 17.5, 20, 22.5, 25, 27.5 and 30° per steel and hardness. Find the lowest angle that plateaus without visible chips.
      2. Inspect the edge with a USB microscope (100–200x) at each checkpoint. Log failure mode (rolling, chipping, wear) with chip count and size.
      3. Measure load in linear feet planed rather than strokes. Fix shaving thickness. Define use cases by wood (for example clear pine vs hard maple with knots or reversing grain).
      4. Alternate irons on the same board to control for board-to-board variation.
      5. Standardize the sharpness test: 3 marked spots on the edge, consistent media and tension, and record the media for each reading.

      Notes

      Design from a toughness requirement

      Set the minimum toughness a use case needs. For each steel the table gives the hardest heat treat that still meets it, the predicted CATRA edge retention at that hardness, and the most acute angle your test results say that toughness supports. The supported angle comes from the Findings tab, filtered by its use case.

      Toughness is Larrin's unnotched subsize Charpy test, read by eye from his toughness vs hardness charts (about ±1 ft-lb). "≥" hardness means the steel beat the requirement even at its hardest tested heat treat. TCC between tested heat treats is interpolated, otherwise shifted from the nearest one by the hardness coefficient. The angle rule assumes more toughness never makes an edge less stable, and it ignores hardness and carbide size, which may also matter.

      How the angle models were fitted

      • The points are read from Larrin's chart of 154CM and CPM-154 knives sharpened from 20° to 56° inclusive, so the steel is held constant.
      • The exponential fit is TCC ∝ e−0.0603 × angle: each degree changes edge retention by about 6%, so a high-wear steel gains or loses more mm per degree than a low-wear one.
      • Larrin's regression uses −17.8 mm per degree. The fitted curve is steeper near 30° (about 29 mm per degree) and flatter at wide angles.
      • Hardness is applied as an offset to the 30° value, then scaled by the angle model.
      • CATRA measures slicing wear in silica-loaded card on a symmetric edge. Planing is a push cut on a single bevel, so treat these as relative wear numbers. A plane iron's honed angle is its inclusive angle. The model covers abrasive wear only, not chipping or rolling.
