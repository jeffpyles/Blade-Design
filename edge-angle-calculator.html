Edge angle and CATRA edge retention

Predicts CATRA Total Cardstock Cut (TCC) at any edge angle. It starts from Larrin Thomas's measured results for 48 steels (all sharpened to 30° inclusive, 400 grit CBN) and applies an angle model fitted to his 154CM edge-angle data. Use it to see how much an angle change is worth compared with a change of steel or hardness.

How the angle models were fitted

Exponential (R² 0.95) Power (R² 0.93) Log (R² 0.91) Linear (R² 0.83)
  • The points are read from Larrin's chart of 154CM and CPM-154 knives sharpened to angles from 20° to 56° inclusive. A single steel family isolates the effect of angle.
  • The exponential fit is TCC ∝ e−0.0603·angle. Each degree changes edge retention by about 6%, so a high-wear steel gains or loses more mm per degree than a low-wear one. The trendlines in Larrin's large multi-steel dataset show the same pattern, which a single linear coefficient can't produce.
  • Larrin's regression uses −17.8 mm per degree. The fitted curve is steeper near 30° (about 29 mm per degree) and flatter at wide angles. His fit was made across many steels and knives at once, which probably dilutes the angle effect.
  • Hardness is applied as an offset to the 30° value (15.8 mm per HRc from his regression), then scaled by the angle model.

Caveats for plane irons

  • CATRA measures slicing wear in silica-loaded card on a symmetric knife edge. Planing is a push cut with a single bevel, so treat these numbers as relative wear resistance, not shavings per sharpening.
  • A plane iron's honed angle is the inclusive angle, so enter a 25° bevel as 25°. Angles below 20° are outside the data and extrapolated.
  • The model only covers abrasive wear. It says nothing about chipping or edge rolling, which is what limits low angles in practice. Pair it with your edge-stability results, using toughness as the constraint.
  • Each of the 48 steels was tested three times and averaged, but repeat runs still scatter, so differences of a few percent between steels aren't meaningful.
